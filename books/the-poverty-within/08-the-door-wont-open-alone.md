The woman who landed Rekha her new job was someone whose name Rekha couldn't even properly recall until the phone rang. Twelve years earlier, before her marriage, Rekha had taken a course in Tally and accounts at a small institute. Her teacher, Mrs. Joshi, was strict, punctual, the kind who kept every student's name in her head. The course ended, the certificate arrived, and Rekha went on her way. For the next twelve years the two of them had almost no contact at all — only the occasional Diwali greeting on a WhatsApp group of Mrs. Joshi's old students, to which Rekha would reply with a smiling sticker. That was the whole of it.

Then Rekha's firm shut down. The small company where she had handled accounts for three years simply closed its doors one morning and was done with it. Now a single salary was dragging two lives along, her daughter's school fees were staring her in the face, and Rekha spent her days uploading her resume to job portals until she was worn out. Nobody answered.

In those very days, one afternoon, Mrs. Joshi called — not on the group, but Rekha directly. "Listen, someone I know who runs a coaching class needs a person to handle accounts, part-time. You're still in that line, aren't you? Shall I give them your number?" Many times afterward it struck Rekha — this work hadn't come to her through her husband, or her brother, or either of the two close friends she spoke to on the phone every day. It came through a teacher from twelve years ago, a woman with whom her entire relationship had shrunk to one sticker a year.

When you hear this, it sounds like Rekha got lucky. But it happens to thousands of people, and it isn't luck — it's a definite rule. In the 1970s, a sociologist named Mark Granovetter asked hundreds of people who had recently changed jobs a single question: how did you learn about this new opportunity? Most of their answers weren't close friends or relatives. They were acquaintances they rarely saw — an old colleague, someone met once at a wedding, a contact good for maybe one phone call a year. Granovetter gave these faint, loose connections a name: "the strength of weak ties."

Once you understand why this happens, you can't un-understand it. Your close friends, your siblings, your daily companions — they all live in the same world you do. They know the same things you know. The same companies, the same troubles, the same handful of opportunities. They love you wholeheartedly, but they have nothing new to offer — because what they're carrying and what you're carrying is the same stock. A distant acquaintance, by contrast, sits in a different company, a different city, a different circle. That same acquaintance can lead you to a door your daily people can't even see. A recent study of more than twenty million people on LinkedIn found exactly this — those whose ties were a little looser, whose acquaintances were a little more spread out, landed more new jobs.

The trouble is that the poverty within cuts a person off from precisely these spread-out connections. When there's money tension hanging over your head, when you're anxious about how the end of the month will get covered, when your body is full of exhaustion — there's simply no room left in the mind for people. Whom to message, whose wedding to attend, whom to drop in on casually — all of it suddenly starts to feel like an unnecessary expense. Of time, of money, of whatever energy is left. A person shrinks automatically. In the end only the most convenient people remain — that is, the people most like you, stuck in the same situation as you.

And then our particular brand of pride adds to it. Why trouble anyone. Why should I now hold out my hand to someone. Getting work by pulling strings feels a little like begging. To a person who has come up in the first generation on their own strength, this feels very close to the heart — we got this far without anyone's support, so why ask for anyone's help now. But this is exactly where the mistake is made. Asking someone for advice, inquiring about an opportunity, isn't begging — it's the everyday give-and-take between people. The one who never asks anyone for anything slowly comes loose from everyone, and a person who has come loose sinks deeper into survival mode. This loneliness doesn't arrive with any fanfare. Nobody shuts a door in your face — it's just that the people who could open new doors quietly drift out of your life. And one day you realize that every single person you know is stuck in exactly the same way you are.

The picture from the opposite side emerges from a vast study by the economist Raj Chetty. Searching for which children from poor homes rise and which stay where they are, he found many things — schools, neighbourhoods, family income. But the single most powerful predictor was just one: cross-class friendship. That is, if a child from a poor home is connected from an early age to people in better circumstances than their own, the chances of that child rising go up sharply. Because those very people bring a new world into the home — new information, new paths, the mere idea that "this too is possible." A friend from a good home casually mentions to a child that such-and-such entrance exam exists, that such-and-such scholarship is available, that there's money in such-and-such career — and just hearing this becomes the first spark in that child's life. What no one around them knew, comes into the home through a friend from a different circle.

So if you want to get out, you have to deliberately go beyond people who are just like you. Keeping alive your acquaintance with old classmates, old colleagues, even old teachers — even if it amounts to no more than one phone call a year. Helping someone before they ask, because people come back easily to the one who gives first. Not being ashamed to ask someone for advice — "how did you manage this, tell me a little" isn't a sign of being lesser; in fact most people enjoy being asked. And finding someone two or four steps ahead of you to be a mentor — someone who will show you the doors your daily circle can't see. Tending to your connections isn't playing some dishonest game; it's staying alive among people. After Rekha started her new job, the first thing she did was go to see Mrs. Joshi — not just to thank her, but to say that if any girl in her new batch needed a place, she would let her know. Once a door opens, it's meant to be kept open, for whoever comes next.

So don't tell yourself you liked this chapter and set the phone down. Open the phone now. Think of one person you've fallen out of touch with over the past few years — an old colleague, a college friend, a teacher. And write them just this much: "It's been ages since we talked, how are you?" Which name will open your next door is something you don't know today, and sitting with sealed lips you will never find out. And you know, too, that if you push this off to tomorrow, tomorrow it will not happen.
