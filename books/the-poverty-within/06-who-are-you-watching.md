Something shifts the moment you step off the bus in your home village. The boy at the tea stall calls out, "The saheb is here!" Your uncle tells the neighbours, "This one's ours — the first to make it into software." A cousin runs his hand over your bike, your grandmother over your head. For three days a kind of intoxication takes hold of you — that you've come a long way, that you've arrived. And then Diwali ends. You come back to the city. You step into the office lift, and somewhere along the way that intoxication falls off you. Here nobody calls you saheb. Here you're just a number, one among many. The same man, in two places — a king in one, an ordinary person in the other. Which is the real one?

Both are real. Because "big" or "small" isn't a fixed thing sitting inside you — it's manufactured out of comparison. It changes depending on who you're standing next to. And here lies a catch that most people never notice: the very yardstick that makes you big back home can be the one that makes you sluggish in the city. Because who you keep comparing yourself with, in your own mind, is what gives your ambition its shape.

Something puzzled economists for a long time. Two people from the same village, in the same circumstances — one struggles and pushes forward, the other stays exactly where he is. The same hardships, the same opportunities. Where exactly is the difference? The answer they landed on has a name — the aspirations window. It means that whatever world is in front of your eyes, only what the people in that world have achieved is what occurs to you to ask for yourself. What someone just like you has managed to do — that much fits inside your imagination. What you've never seen up close, it doesn't even cross your mind to want. Ambition feels intensely personal, but it is largely social — the people around you are quietly deciding it.

The trouble with the village yardstick is that the distance is too small. The truth is, you've already gone ahead of those friends. A comparison made by looking downward is always pleasant — "look where I came from and look where I've reached" is a warming thought, a proud one. But that same thought quietly kills your hunger. If you're already ahead of everyone, there's nothing left to pull you forward. You'll stay on the same salary, in the same position, for five years, and you won't even feel the prick of it — because twice a year you go back to the village and get your dose of being royalty, and your mind stays calm on that alone. You get contentment. Only the growth stops.

Now look at the exact opposite end. You lie down in bed at night and open the feed. A boy in his twenties is earning ten lakh a month. Someone is laughing into the camera from a flat in Dubai. Someone's fourth car was just delivered. You keep watching, the bluish glow of the screen on your face, and only one feeling is left inside — "this is never going to happen for me in this lifetime." Here the distance is so enormous that you can't even see the road. And where the road can't be seen, a person doesn't take even the first step. Researchers call this stuckness aspirations failure. The startling part is that there's no outer wall stopping you here — what stops you is your own badly calibrated yardstick. Either too close, or too far. Both extremes nail a person to the spot.

So where does real hunger come from? It comes from people who are one or two steps ahead of you. Close enough that you can say, "this person was just like me, if he could do it then so can I" — and far enough ahead that looking at them makes you want to stretch. Not so far below that you'll sit back at ease, and not so far above that you simply throw up your hands.

Suppose a new team lead joins the office. Just three years older than you, from the same background, out of a Marathi-medium school just like yours. But in the time in between he's done a certification, earned a little money from a side project, and now he's negotiating for a better role. At first it might feel slightly awkward. But as you talk to him every day, something happens — a road that was invisible before suddenly becomes visible. Which certification, where to begin, who to talk to — all of it becomes graspable, concrete. In the village yardstick, you were a king. In this man's yardstick, you're someone just starting out. And it's exactly this second yardstick that moves you.

Look carefully at the difference. The ten-lakh boy on the feed — dreamlike, a stranger, out of reach. And this team lead sitting beside you — familiar, reachable. Two forms of the very same desire: one freezes you, the other gets you walking. The difference isn't in them — the difference is in who you keep your eyes on.

And here is the place of escape. Who you look at isn't decided by fate — it's largely down to your own choice. Who decides your yardstick today? Most often, the feed decides it. The accounts you follow, the people whose reels make your thumb stop, the WhatsApp group where you sit silent and scroll — without your noticing, these become your aspirations window. Which means every night you yourself are deciding who you'll compare yourself with tomorrow — you just don't realise it.

So do it knowingly. The account that only makes you feel defeated while showing you no road — unfollow it, this very day. The endless scroll of unreachable people isn't inspiration, it's self-torture. And stop measuring yourself against the village yardstick too — it gives contentment, not hunger. Instead, find at least one person who's a step or two ahead, get close to them, ask them questions, watch how they work. If there's no such person around you, go looking deliberately — in a course, an online community, at your workplace. Choose who you look at every day — because if you don't choose that yardstick, someone has already chosen it for you.
