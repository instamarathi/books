Try a strange exercise. The sixty-year-old you — wrinkles, white hair, slow feet — who is that man to you? "Me, obviously," you'll say at once, out loud. But peer deep inside your mind, and does that man feel like "me," or like some familiar-yet-unfamiliar old man living at the far end of the lane? For most of us the answer is unsettling. That man feels close to us, but he doesn't feel like "me." He's a little bit of a stranger.

And that's exactly where the trouble lies. Who sacrifices today for a stranger?

Psychology has a lovely experiment about this. A researcher named Hal Hershfield showed people a digitally aged image of their own face — the same face, but wrinkled, tired, old. Their own face, the one they have never once seen in a mirror. Then he asked: would you spend the money in hand today, or move it toward long-term savings? Those who had actually looked their old face in the eye were more willing to steer money toward the future. Before they saw the face, that old man was a hazy idea; after they saw it, he became a real person. And for a real person, we automatically behave a little better.

So the question was never really about money. It was about recognition.

This is exactly the relationship that scarcity severs. When money is short, you see the world through a narrow window — next month's rent, the date the fees are due, the groceries running low. That's all that fits in the window. The window is "today, this week at most." Five years, twenty years — it all dissolves into the fog beyond. Psychology calls this present bias, and scarcity sharpens it. The strange part is that a person turns his back on the future at precisely the moment he needs the future most. And once the future goes hazy, the person living in that future goes hazy too. He becomes a stranger. And it doesn't trouble the mind much to pick a stranger's pocket.

That's exactly what had happened to Mangesh. A bank SIP form had sat in a diary in his cupboard for a whole year — a friend had filled it out and brought it over, just two thousand rupees a month, everything ready, nothing left but to sign. The form drifted somewhere between the electricity bill and the school receipts. When someone asked, he'd laugh and say, "We'll see later, things are a bit tight right now." This "tight" had been going on for four years, even though his salary had gone up twice in between. He'd put an online course in his cart three times and removed it three times. The reason was the same — we'll see later.

This "we'll see later" wasn't laziness. Mangesh worked hard, took care of his home. It was just that in his mind, tomorrow's Mangesh was a dim figure, one for whom dipping into today's wallet didn't feel worth it. That man didn't feel like "me" — and so he kept on waiting.

In Mangesh's case, it was a simple question that shook him loose. One Sunday evening, handing him a cup of tea, his wife said casually, "What do you think we'll be doing when we're sixty?" And for the first time, he actually stopped. He didn't change the subject with the usual "we'll see." He closed his eyes and tried to paint the picture — not in broad strokes, but in detail. Sixty-year-old Mangesh. Knees that ache. One dawn a sharp pain in the chest, an admission to the hospital. A bill arrives at the counter. Who's going to pay it? Will he hold out his hand to his newly married daughter's husband? Or to a son tangled up in his own EMIs? That old man lies on the bed waiting for someone's help — and the help was supposed to come from forty-year-old Mangesh. The one who had the form, who only had to sign.

The picture stood up so clearly that it made him uneasy. That unease was the real change. The next day he dug the form out of the diary, signed it, took it to the bank. Two thousand rupees. Not a big sum. But the form was no longer for an unknown future — it was for that old man on the bed, the one he had now given a face.

Anyone can do this, and it doesn't take an app or any software. Paint a picture of your seventy-year-old self in your mind — not hazy, solid. What time does that man wake up in the morning? Who makes his tea? Is the house back in the village still there, or did it have to be sold? Who comes to visit at festival time? And then, honestly, write that man a letter. On paper, with a pen, beginning "Dear seventy-year-old me." Tell him what you're doing for him today, and where you're falling short, ask for a little forgiveness. It will feel strange — why would a person write a letter to himself. But that very strangeness is what makes the man real. And once he's real, setting a little aside for him today suddenly doesn't feel so heavy.

Of course, not all short-term thinking comes from this trick of the mind. Sometimes a person doesn't think about tomorrow because, today, he genuinely has nothing left in hand. If your pocket is empty at month's end, the advice to "write a letter to your future self" is merely galling. You can't save what isn't there. So this path doesn't walk alone — it walks alongside the financial buffer from the previous chapter. First a little breathing room, space to draw breath. And then, in that room, building a relationship with tomorrow's self. Two separate things; one doesn't do the other's work.

But for those who have a little room and yet keep saying "we'll see later" while the form sits in the diary — for them, the problem isn't money. It's recognition. That man of tomorrow is somewhere today, not yet born but on his way, and he's counting on you alone. Give him a name. Give him a face. He won't become close in a single night — that narrow window will keep narrowing again and again, and "we'll see later" will keep rising to your lips. Only now, you'll know that beyond the window someone is standing, and he's depending on you alone.
