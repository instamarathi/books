It is seven in the evening. Meera, nine years old, is standing in the kitchen doorway with a sheet of paper from school in her hand. There is a two-day trip — bus, lodging, food, all of it adding up to a fixed amount. She holds the paper out, but as she asks, her voice drops, her shoulders curl inward, and finally she almost whispers, "...it's fine if I don't go, you know. It's no big deal." A nine-year-old girl, already taught to retreat before she has even asked.

There is money in the house. The trip amount is hardly a burden. So where did that hesitation come from? Nobody has told her "we can't afford it." But children don't learn from words — they learn from the language of the body. A small tightening in her mother's face while looking at a bill. The shift in her father's tone as he said, "this much?" The hushed money-talk over dinner, the kind children aren't meant to hear but always do. Even in a comfortable home this pull seeps into the next generation — because it lives in the air, not in the accounts.

And it is exactly here that everything we have been talking about throughout this book finally reveals its meaning. To climb out of survival mode is, of course, for yourself — but the deepest reason is sitting right in front of you, in the curled shoulders of that nine-year-old girl.

So the question that remains is: how do you climb out? And here one thing has to be kept clear, because it is the spine of the entire journey: money alone does not do it, and the mind alone does not do it either. Only when the two move together does the escape hold.

Research suggests exactly this, and it suggests it in an amusing way — from both sides at once. In Kenya, an organization called GiveDirectly gave poor families money directly, with no conditions attached — and there was a genuinely measurable rise in people's mental wellbeing, on the order of a quarter of a standard deviation, which in studies like these is considered significant. So even money that simply lands in your hand does work on the mind; the constant weight on your head lifts a little. But on the other side, in some other cash-transfer studies, money arrived and yet made little difference to wellbeing. Both findings are true, and together their meaning is simple — money opens the door, but walking through it is our own work.

That is why we worked with both hands. With one hand we built a little material safety — three months of expenses set aside, so that the body doesn't have to brace and stand at attention for every small daily shock. And with the other hand, the inner, slow work — slowing the breath to teach the body that "there's no emergency now"; treating hope not as a feeling but as a skill that can be practised; changing who we compare ourselves to each day, because keep looking at the wrong people and even what's in your hand starts to feel like too little; and slowly setting down the old identity of "I'm basically from a poor family." Neither of these is enough on its own. Just money, or just "positive thinking" — both stay half-finished.

And this work is not fast. There is no switch you press somewhere to shut survival mode off. It is daily, modest, often tedious work — and real all the same. There is one more thing to admit here: if this habit took root in childhood, in a home of hunger or constant stress, then that old setting lodged in the body may perhaps never be fully erased. And that is all right. The goal is not to wipe it out — the goal is to hold it, to manage it. That grip on the chest will return now and then; and when it does, instead of blaming yourself, simply being able to recognize it — "ah, there it is again" — and turning back to the breath, that is the real progress. Not the grip never coming, but being able to recognize it when it does.

So beyond all of this, what is thriving? It is certainly not that endless race of "more, more." It is not the scramble to reach the benchmarks of the rich, because that race is deliberately built to have no end. Thriving is simpler than that. Being able to spend on what genuinely matters without guilt — being able to hand over the money for your daughter's trip without flinching. Being able to take a real risk because there's a little cushion behind you — a new line of work, a new beginning, one big attempt that, even if it fails, won't bring everything crashing down. And most important of all — being able to enjoy what you have, instead of guarding it for dear life. "Enough" is not the final stop. It is a place to take a leap from. Life begins there; it does not end there.

And here we have to come back, once more, to that nine-year-old girl. You can give children a home, you can give them a school, you can keep numbers in the bank — but something larger than all of that is in your hands to give. You can give them a nervous system that learned, very early on, that "we are safe." The more you loosen your own pull, the less of it reaches them. Money can run out, an investment can collapse; but a mind that has learned "the world is not that dangerous" stays with a person for life. That is the true inheritance — invisible in the accounts, but lasting for generations.

What will happen that evening? Perhaps Meera's parents won't just say "go." Perhaps they'll pause a moment and say, "Lovely. Definitely go. Don't you worry about the money — that's my job, not yours." A small sentence. And as they say it, that old tightening will still be felt somewhere inside them — for an instant the thought "this much money?" will flicker through the mind even so. But they didn't let that thought travel down to the girl's shoulders, that's all. A chain, for the length of one sentence, broke.

This is the escape. Not perfect, not all at once, and certainly not forever. That pull will keep coming back now and then — on some Sunday, in front of some bill, sometimes for no reason at all. Let it come. Now, when it arrives, at least you know what it is, where it came from, and what to do with it. The numbers in the account were perhaps always enough; now let the mind, too, slowly come to believe it — without hurrying, one breath at a time. It is enough. And whatever lies onward from here is not merely surviving — it is living.
