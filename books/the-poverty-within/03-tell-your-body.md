It's two in the morning. You're lying in bed, but sleep has slipped off somewhere. Your jaw is clenched — you don't even know how long it's been like that. Your heart is racing in your chest with a strange urgency, as if something has just happened or is about to. Your shoulders are pulled up toward your ears. There's a nameless tightness in your stomach. And you lie there with your eyes open, staring into the dark, wondering — what happened? What's tomorrow? Office, the usual meeting, your son's homework. That's all. No real danger anywhere. The door is locked, the house is quiet, the only sound outside is the occasional rickshaw passing. And yet the body is behaving as if someone is standing at the door.

This pounding doesn't come from your head. Your head is reasoning with you — "Look, everything's fine, go to sleep now." But the body doesn't listen. And there's a reason it doesn't. The body has spent years learning a language, and that language isn't made of words. In a childhood home where the month-end numbers never quite added up, where the suppressed strain in your parents' voices is something you can't clearly remember anymore but which has settled somewhere deep inside — that's where the language was learned. Back then it was a need. A body that stayed constantly on guard was protecting you then. The only problem is that the danger left long ago, and the body stayed right where it was, standing watch in that old night.

In research this has a name — allostatic load — the wear that slowly accumulates on the body from staying perpetually on alert. This isn't just an idea; it can be measured directly through blood pressure, hormones, sleep patterns. Like a car whose engine has been left running for years — nothing shows on the outside, but something inside keeps wearing down. And this is exactly where there's something reassuring. The body learned in one direction, and it can learn in the other direction too. The same plasticity of the brain that wedged this vigilance in so firmly is the plasticity that can loosen it again, little by little. It doesn't happen at once, and it doesn't happen through words. The body has to be told in its own language.

So who tells the body that the tiger is gone?

First of all — and the thing that's talked about least — people. Safe, supportive relationships. This isn't just about avoiding stress. For the body to settle again after stress has come, the presence of another calm person is needed — that's a foundational requirement. Sometimes at night you're lying there sweating, wide awake, and someone beside you simply holds your hand and sits there — saying nothing. What that one touch does, no amount of "positive thinking" can do. The body senses another calm body before it registers any words, and that sensing says only one thing — you're not fighting this alone anymore.

We have to talk about sleep, because this is where the real obstacle lies for most people. Somewhere inside, sleep has become a thing to be ashamed of. "Who has time to sleep" — we say it almost with pride. A person who sleeps early is lazy, unambitious — that belief is lodged somewhere in us. Exhaustion feels like a badge of hard work. But a tired, sleep-deprived body stays permanently on alert. On the night after you've slept only four hours, that two-a.m. pounding returns without fail. Through the night the body quietly repairs the day's wear. Cut that time short, and the wear stays as it is, and fresh wear piles on top.

There's one detail about movement that's quite beautiful. The hippocampus, which shrinks under prolonged stress — that very part of the brain — is where physical movement produces new cells and new plasticity. Meaning the very region that constant vigilance wore down can recover again through nothing more than walking. You don't need a gym for this, you don't need a marathon. Half an hour in the morning, a simple walk on the street near your house, is enough. At first this will feel like "wasting time" — "I'm supposed to just walk around for that long?" But after a few weeks one thing becomes clear — on the days you walked, the body is a little less tense that night. The proof comes from the body itself.

And beyond this, mindfulness that pays deliberate attention to the breath, and time spent in nature — trees, open sky, the soft early light of morning. These sound so simple that we don't take them seriously. But for a body stuck in constant vigilance, that very simplicity is the gentle medicine.

There's one thing that, if it isn't said plainly here, would turn this chapter into just another self-help blog. All of these things work — but their effect isn't all that large, and there's still disagreement about them in the research. In one carefully conducted eight-week trial, it turned out that meditation and yoga didn't produce markedly different or markedly better results than a simple stress-education class — and they didn't move the allostatic load marker at all. So the expectation that "ten minutes of meditation a day will transform everything inside" isn't true. This is hard to say, but there's no point hiding it.

Do them anyway — because these aren't guarantees, they're supports. For a body stuck in constant vigilance, these things help you carry the load overall; not as a magic button, but like an ordinary daily meal. And here there's one firm rule — not intensity, but consistency. Twenty minutes of walking every day is more useful to the body than two hours of sweating once a week. Because the message "the danger has passed" can't be delivered just once. It has to be given daily, slowly, again and again — not in one big jolt, but through hundreds of small habits.

So next time you wake at two in the morning and your chest starts to race, don't immediately fight that pounding. It's your old protector; it did its job once. It's just that now you have to tell it, gently — the tiger left long ago. It may not listen right away. Maybe nothing changes that night. But in the morning you'll go for a walk, at night you'll lie down on time, and you'll sit holding someone's hand — and through these small daily things the body will slowly, at its own pace, forget that old language. Not completely, never completely. But enough.
