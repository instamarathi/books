Surekha's cursor was hovering over the "submit" button. On the screen sat a completed rental form for a small kitchen unit — eight thousand a month, close to home, room to install an oven. For two years she had been baking cakes out of her own house; the orders came in steadily now, people knew her by name. The next step was obvious. She had typed in her name, her address, her phone number — and then, at the very last button, she shut the laptop. "Let me just think it over," she told herself. This was the third time.

The reason that form refused to let Surekha finish it had nothing to do with the cake business itself. It lay in three other things she kept smouldering on the side. Two evenings a week she did data-entry at an office — barely any money, but she was "still working." Her sister had left the receptionist's chair at her clinic open with a standing "come whenever you feel like it." And somewhere there was a faint plan — the whole family moving to her husband's village, where costs were lower, and then they'd see. She didn't want a single one of these. But she hadn't cancelled a single one either. She kept all three burning like low embers.

Because as long as those three paths stayed alive, the cake business could never become "the one thing." If the shop didn't work out, there was a sentence ready to speak — "look, I wasn't relying on it alone." Every unchosen option was a pre-prepared excuse. And the real job of that excuse wasn't to sell cakes; it was to save her from one thing — the verdict. Because a thing left half-done never reaches a verdict. It never passes, but it never fails either. And to a mind that has carried the fear of "you are not enough" all its life, staying permanently half-finished feels easier than hearing the word "failed."

These low embers have a name — the escape hatch. The back door left open. It arrives dressed in sensible-sounding sentences: "I'm just trying this out," "if it doesn't work, the clinic's there," "we could always move to the village." It sounds like wisdom. But its real job is to pardon the failure in advance — and a failure that has already been pardoned is never fully yours, so you can never learn from it either. There's an old story — a general who landed on a shore burned the boats that could have carried his men back, so that the soldiers' attention couldn't drift backwards and turned entirely to what lay ahead. Whether the story is true or a legend, set that aside; its point is simple. A person with half his life turned toward the path of escape never fully commits to the task in front of him.

But there is a mistake that's very easy to make here, and it costs dearly. Someone hears "burn all your back doors" and quits their job the next morning, pours every rupee they have into one thing, and calls themselves "brave." That isn't commitment. So the difference between two things has to be understood clearly — money set aside as a cushion and an escape hatch are two different things, even though both feel like "a backup behind you."

The six-to-eight months of expenses Surekha had set aside were not an escape hatch. Their job was different — to guarantee that even if the shop ran at a loss, the household would keep running, the children's fees wouldn't stop. That money makes failure survivable. You don't cut it; on the contrary, jumping without it is foolishness. The escape hatch does exactly the opposite. It doesn't make failure bearable, it gives you permission to avoid failure. Saved-up money says, "even if you fall you'll live, so jump without fear." The escape hatch whispers in your ear, "don't jump at all — the clinic's behind you, keep looking that way." One pushes you forward; the other keeps you forever hovering between three paths.

So the order must never be reversed. First make the bet survivable, then close the door. A person with nothing under their feet can't fully commit to anything — and shouldn't, because for them committing fully means a straight wager on drowning. Surekha had gathered that money first, which is exactly why the second step was now possible. One Sunday evening she did the thing she had avoided for three years — she put out all three embers, but choosing one at a time. She phoned her sister and said it plainly: "No, I'm going to do this full-time now." She told the data-entry people, "I won't be coming from next month." And the same submit button on that form, on the third try, she pressed.

The hardest part was something else entirely — she told people. Her husband, two close friends, and even a WhatsApp status: "Got my own kitchen now. The next year, only this." Saying it was frightening, because now if she turned back, everyone would see. But that was precisely the point. Closing the door isn't just a decision in your head; once you place it outside, in front of others, it takes on weight. The escape hatch was shut.

And a strange thing happened. The hazy, scattered worry that had sat spread across her chest for years now shrank into one small, clean fear. And that fear was light. Because the question was no longer "what exactly should I do, which path?"; the question was only "what do I do tomorrow morning?" The energy that had been divided among three half-open doors now flowed down a single path — and that was when she realised how much of her strength had been spent simply on keeping the escape routes alive.

How far Surekha's kitchen went from there isn't the point here. The point is that, for the first time, the verdict on one thing felt entirely her own — if success came, it was hers; if failure came, that was hers too, not a punishment but a step toward learning. Up to here we have built a cushion behind us, closed the back door on one single thing, and turned scattered fear into the focus of one direction. But once all your strength is aimed at a single path, a quieter question steps forward — one there was never any room to ask in survival mode. Not whether you can chase the big thing, but whether, when it finally arrives, you can ever let yourself enjoy it. And it's here, in the last stretch, that this story begins to turn toward its close.
