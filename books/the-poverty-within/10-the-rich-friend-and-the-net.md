Do you remember that one friend — the one who skipped lectures in college, casually picked up a KT in whichever subjects bored him, and was the first in the final year to declare, chest puffed out, "I'm going to start something of my own"? Let's call him Ameya. Ameya's first idea fell flat within six months. He shrugged, started another, that one died too, and somewhere in the third he found his footing. And you? You passed every paper, met every deadline, and the first job that landed in your hands straight out of campus you gripped with both fists — because the mere thought of letting it go to look around sent a shiver down your spine.

The years went by. A question kept needling you from the inside, one you never said out loud to anyone: Ameya wasn't smarter than me, and he certainly wasn't more hardworking — so where did all that nerve come from, and why didn't I have it?

To find the answer you kept peering into your own temperament. It isn't there. It was under Ameya's feet.

Behind him stood something he would never even have mentioned — because he didn't feel it strongly enough to mention. His father owned the house they lived in. There was a little farmland back in the village. And the idea that "whatever happens, the boy won't go hungry" was so taken for granted in that home that it never even needed to be spoken. For Ameya, an attempt failing meant an experiment failing — a lesson, an anecdote he could one day recount with a laugh. That if he leapt from a height there would be someone below to catch him was something his body had known since birth.

For you it wasn't like that. For you, quitting your job wasn't an experiment; it was sliding back to the very place your family had spent years straining at the net to climb out of. Your failure wasn't only yours — it was washing away your parents' days and nights of effort. So you didn't take the big swing. You placed small, safe bets. And listen — that wasn't cowardice, it was precise good sense. The one with a net below flies high, because for him the cost of falling is a scratch. The one with no net clings tight to the ground, because for him falling means everything ends. This was never a contest of courage. It was a difference in the ground beneath your feet.

In card games there's a phrase — "playing with house money." When the money on the table isn't from your own pocket, a person places a big bet easily; even losing doesn't sting much. Ameya played with house money his whole life, and you wagered every rupee out of your own sweat. Two people sitting at the same table played differently — but the difference wasn't in the cards in their hands, it was in what was kept under the chair.

This really is unfair, and admit that it is unfair. Some are born carrying this net for free; others have to weave it their whole lives. There's no point holding it against Ameya — he never asked for the net, it simply fell to his share. But comparing yourself to his luck and stamping yourself with "I'm just a coward by nature" — that is the real injustice, and you're committing it not against Ameya but against yourself.

Now the real twist begins, and it's on your side.

Because today, after all these years, there is a net under your feet. Some savings have built up. You have a stable job. Maybe there's a house in your own name, or the EMI is nearly done. Lay it out on paper — what Ameya was handed for free at birth, you have reached on your own, through your own labour, or close to it. The floor is ready. You built it yourself.

But here something strange happens. The floor is ready, and yet the mind, when placing a bet, behaves as if there's still a void below. Looking at the balance on your phone at night, the number says it plainly — "even if I lose my job tomorrow, nothing will happen for six months." You read that number over and over. And still the old shout comes from inside — don't move, stay stuck right where you are, everything could vanish in an instant. The evidence in front of your eyes says one thing, and your body screams the exact opposite.

**What Ameya was given at birth wasn't just a net of money — it was one in the mind too.** His nervous system knew from the start that someone was below. Your nervous system was schooled somewhere else, where the only lesson was: no one will catch you, fend for yourself. That lesson went so deep that even after the net has been woven beneath your feet, the body refuses to trust it.

So what work is left? Not the work of weaving the net afresh — you wove that long ago. The work is making the mind feel the net. And fear doesn't leave through argument; it only loosens through evidence. So take small leaps. Not a full venture all at once — but a course, a side project, one conversation with your manager about your pay, or one "no" you never had the nerve to say before. Every time you take a little swing and your body learns from experience that there really is a net below, one thread of felt safety gets woven. What Ameya got for free at birth, that inner security, you can now earn with your own hands, one thread at a time.

Keep the humility about Ameya to the end. He got a head start, and that really was unfair — but don't let that disparity curdle into bitterness. Because the net that someone placed ready in his hands, you wove yourself. It's only that for so many years you kept looking up, which is why it still hasn't met your eyes.

Look down. It's right there.
