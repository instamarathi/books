For some people, the idea that trying hard doesn't change much isn't a feeling — it's a settled fact. As fixed as the weather. "It's just not in our fate" — they don't say it in anger; they say it calmly, the way someone says "it's going to rain tomorrow." And the most troublesome part is this: the sentence doesn't even feel to them like an opinion of theirs — it feels like a description of reality.

At a family lunch, someone casually asked Sandeep, "Why don't you start something of your own? You understand all of it." Sandeep pushed his plate forward and said, "People like us can't do that." It wasn't a considered answer. It came out on its own — the way, when someone asks you what two plus two is, the answer is already waiting in your mouth.

In psychology this state has a name — learned helplessness. Where year after year you keep moving and the result barely shifts — the rain doesn't come on time, in the office a file won't move forward without a connection, you work hard and the chance still goes to someone else — the brain drills one lesson in until it's set firm: "My actions have no effect on the result." Once that lesson lands, even when an opportunity comes and stands right in front of you, the hand doesn't reach out. Because the voice inside has already spoken.

We treat hope as a temperament — "that man is naturally hopeful, I'm naturally not," as if it were settled at birth. But researchers look at hope differently. They break it into three separate parts and — this is the important thing — all three of those parts appear to be movable from the outside. One part is aspirations, letting yourself want something again. One part is agency, the belief that "my actions affect the result." And one part is pathways, the actual road that gets you there. The story of each of these is not the same, so there's no point lining them up as one thing.

The first part — letting yourself want — has a delicate trouble built into it. Sandeep won't even let the wish for a business arise, because if the wish arises and then isn't fulfilled, it will hurt. So the mind keeps the switch of desire turned off in advance — a cheap way to avoid pain. Can that closed switch be nudged open from outside? A study from the Oaxaca region of Mexico suggests something here. It looked at 601 indigenous women — for most of them, "this is how it will always be" was simply taken for granted. Some of them were shown just a 35-minute documentary: women like themselves, from the same circumstances, who had lifted their lives a little. Along with it, a small exercise of setting a goal. That was all — no loan, no training, no big project. And yet, in the measurements, their aspirations and their overall hope were seen to rise.

Hearing this, one shouldn't leap too far too fast. The effects in this study were modest, not large, and mostly measured short-term. Nobody becomes an entrepreneur overnight from watching one documentary. But the direction is clear: when an example like yourself, someone who has climbed a little, comes before your eyes, the closed switch inside shifts a bit — and the cost of this is close to zero.

Still, the wish coming back doesn't mean a step forward gets taken. Between the wish and the action stands that second part — agency, the belief that "I can do this" — and this is where the real work is, so let's linger a little. We tend to think this belief comes from a speech: someone puts a hand on your back, says "you can do it, believe in yourself," and there it is. But the psychologist Bandura showed the opposite. He called it self-efficacy, and said this belief is built not out of words but out of evidence. The brain is like an old, weathered bookkeeper — it doesn't trust the assurances it hears, it only looks at the record. You actually finished one small task — the bookkeeper makes an entry in the ledger. You did another — another entry. As such entries accumulate, at some point the bookkeeper changes the figure on his own: "People like us can do this." That is, hope isn't poured in from above; it's built from below, on one small finished thing at a time.

Let's come back to Sandeep. Giving him one more piece of advice will do nothing — he has heard advice his whole life, and the bookkeeper doesn't record advice. His cousin Rajesh came from the same village, his English is rougher than Sandeep's, and today he has set up a small catering operation. Rajesh is, for Sandeep, that Oaxaca documentary — "someone just like me, and he managed it," a piece of direct evidence right before the eyes. But agency isn't built just from watching an example; for that you need an entry under your own name. So keep the next step so small it feels laughable. No business plan. Just — "this Sunday, meet Rajesh and ask him how he got started."

Sandeep meets Rajesh. One entry. Later he sits down and works out a small calculation for himself — whether there's demand for such a service in his area. One entry. Then one Saturday, as a trial, he takes one small order and completes it. Another entry. None of these steps is large in itself. But with each completed step that old lesson — "my actions have no effect on the result" — fades a little, and a new record builds in its place. This isn't positive thinking; it's gathering evidence.

And here the third part — pathways — comes in on its own, through the back door. After talking to Rajesh, the picture grows a little clearer: "where to get the first order from, where to source the supplies, which other road to take if one road closes." The road isn't laid out first and then walked; the next turn becomes visible as you walk.

So if, deep inside, it feels like "it's just not in my fate," that is not a settled fact about you — it is a lesson that old circumstances drilled into you, and a drilled-in lesson can be scrubbed out and rewritten again. Let yourself want a little again. Find an example like yourself, someone who has climbed. And decide on one step small enough that you can actually finish it tomorrow — your first entry from today. The result will be modest, it will be slow, and the first few steps may fail too. But even a failed step becomes an entry in the ledger — "at least I reached out." That old bookkeeper is slow; he won't change his mind over one or two entries. Still, the ledger is open now — and the first entry, from today, is in your own hands.
